01 — Research

Understanding Detroit before drawing a single line

Before any mark is made, the city has to be understood. Detroit carries contradictions: industrial decay and creative resilience, blue-collar roots and boundary-pushing culture. Its identity is not one thing — and neither should a hockey team's brand be.

Research covered two parallel tracks: the history of Detroit as a city and the history of hockey in Detroit. Both informed every decision that followed.

Detroit is a city defined by reinvention. From the auto industry that shaped American manufacturing, to Motown that gave the world a new sound, to a contemporary art and design scene that continues to emerge from its own ruins — Detroit refuses to be only what it was.

That tension — grit and grace, legacy and ambition — became the emotional core of this brand.

🏙️
History of Detroit
Founded in 1701 on the Detroit River, the city became the center of American automotive manufacturing in the early 20th century. The Big Three — Ford, GM, Chrysler — built not just cars but a working-class culture of pride and craft. Motown Records (est. 1959) transformed it into a cultural capital. Post-industrial challenges reshaped the city, yet a renaissance in arts, food, and design has redefined Detroit for a new generation.
Industrial Motown Automotive Renaissance Great Lakes
🏒
History of Detroit Hockey
The Detroit Red Wings (est. 1926) are one of the Original Six NHL franchises and holders of 11 Stanley Cup championships. Detroit earned the nickname "Hockeytown" — not a marketing slogan, but a lived identity. The octopus toss at Joe Louis Arena, the retired jersey banners, the Gordie Howe legacy: hockey is civic religion here. The PWHL expansion represents a new chapter in that legacy, finally giving women's hockey a permanent home in one of the sport's most passionate cities.
Red Wings Hockeytown Original Six PWHL Joe Louis Arena
Key Design Insight

Detroit doesn't perform toughness — it lives it. The brand must feel earned, not imposed. Imagery of steel, rivers, and motion rather than generic sports aggression.

"

Detroit doesn't need to borrow toughness from anywhere else. It is the source.

— Research conclusion, brand brief

03 — Sketches & Ideation

Exploring directions before committing to one

Sketching serves one purpose: generating more ideas than you'll ever use. No concept is too literal or too abstract at this stage. The goal is volume, then ruthless editing.

Directions explored included a stylized Detroit River wave form, geometric interpretations of the Ambassador Bridge, letterform-based marks, and animal mascot concepts rooted in Michigan's natural iconography.

06 — Logo & Wordmark

From sketch to final mark

The logo development process moved from the six sketch directions to three refined candidates, then to a single refined mark. Each iteration tested legibility at jersey scale, clarity at favicon scale, and expression of the brand's core tension.

The selected direction carries the full weight of the brand: it is rooted in Detroit's visual vocabulary, works in two colors, and reads clearly from the upper deck of a hockey arena. It is not delicate — and it doesn't need to be.

08 — Brand Guidelines

How the brand is used — and protected

A brand is only as strong as its consistency. These guidelines define how the visual system is applied across contexts — ensuring the identity holds from a 40-foot arena banner to a 16px mobile favicon.

Rules exist not to be restrictive, but to preserve the intent behind every design decision. Clear space, color hierarchy, and type pairing rules prevent the system from drifting over time.

Do Logo Usage
  • Use the primary mark on approved background colors only (navy, red, cream)
  • Maintain clear space equal to the height of the "D" on all sides
  • Use the one-color version when printing on a single-color surface
  • Scale the wordmark proportionally using the locked lockup file
  • Use the secondary badge mark for small-scale applications (under 1 inch)
Don't Logo Usage
  • Do not stretch, skew, or distort the logo mark
  • Do not place the logo on a patterned background without a solid backing
  • Do not use unapproved colors — the Steel Fog swatch is never used for the logo
  • Do not outline or add drop shadows to the mark
  • Do not recreate the logo in unauthorized typefaces
Do Color & Type
  • Use Detroit Red as the dominant primary in all marketing contexts
  • Pair display type with generous white space — let it breathe
  • Use Lake Cream as a neutral backdrop for editorial layouts
  • Reserve Foundry Gold for detail and trim — never as a body color
  • Maintain a 4.5:1 contrast ratio for all text and background combinations
Don't Color & Type
  • Do not use red type on a navy background — insufficient contrast
  • Do not mix display and body type at the same hierarchy level
  • Do not introduce colors outside the approved five-color palette
  • Do not use Steel Fog as a primary surface color — it's a supporting neutral only
  • Do not set body copy in the display/collegiate typeface
