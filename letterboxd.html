Designing for Translation on Letterboxd

A UX writing case study examining clarity, tone, and inclusivity in Letterboxd's interface.

Role Lead UX Writer
Tools Figma, Photoshop, Letterboxd
Team 1 Designer
Timeline September – December 2025 (8 weeks)
Overview

A global community, speaking one language

Letterboxd has become a beloved hub for film lovers to log movies, write reviews, and connect with cinephiles worldwide. The platform brands itself as a "global social network for grass-roots film discussion and discovery," yet it currently exists only in English.

Users post reviews in dozens of languages, but without translation or localization features, these diverse voices remain isolated from the wider community they're part of.

Source: Letterboxd's Countries & Languages page, showcasing the range of countries its database of films is produced in — letterboxd.com/countries

The Problem

Before translation, clarity

Before Letterboxd can offer translation tools or localized versions, its microcopy needs to be simplified and standardized. The current voice is passionate and reference-heavy — it captures the energy of film culture, but makes translation difficult. Ambiguous phrasing, idioms, and insider references can't easily or accurately be localized for global audiences.

Research & Discovery

Understanding the audience

Explored Letterboxd's interface and community through the lens of global accessibility.

The People

To understand how Letterboxd's copy impacts real users, I developed two personas that represent key segments of the platform's audience — one a native English speaker, the other a native Spanish speaker. Both illustrate how voice and tone shape accessibility, clarity, and engagement across different cultural and linguistic contexts.

J
Johnny
Native English Speaker

Johnny is fluent in Letterboxd's playful, reference-heavy voice. He gets the idioms, catches the film-nerd jokes, and feels right at home in the community's inside language — the tone is part of why he keeps coming back.

S
Sara
Native Spanish Speaker

Sara is just as passionate about film as Johnny, but she navigates Letterboxd entirely in her second language. English idioms, cultural references, and ambiguous phrasing occasionally slow her down or leave her guessing at meaning the interface never intended to hide.

Johnny and Sara together reveal how Letterboxd's strong, cinephile-driven voice builds community, but can unintentionally exclude users unfamiliar with English idioms or film references. These personas inform opportunities to balance enthusiasm with clarity, and style with inclusivity.

Voice & Tone Analysis

Auditing the microcopy

Audited Letterboxd's microcopy to identify patterns in tone, clarity, and inclusivity. The analysis reveals that Letterboxd maintains a playful, cinephile-driven voice that fosters community and enthusiasm for film. Yet tonal inconsistencies weaken clarity and inclusivity — the dialogue provides little to no guidance or reassurance in instances where it may be needed most, such as empty states or error messages.

These findings provide a foundation for understanding how Letterboxd's language choices affect user interactions.

Analysis conducted using Torrey Podmajersky's voice and tone framework.

Recommendations
Recommendation 01

Clear, actionable labels to support decision-making

I redesigned critical labels across the platform to reduce ambiguity and support immediate comprehension. The updated system replaces vague noun phrases with plain-language, verb-led options.

These changes create stronger alignment between intention and action, improve trust in system feedback, and make the UI more accessible for multilingual users. The clarity also ensures more accurate translation during future localization.

Recommendation 02

Empty states that guide, not confuse

Letterboxd uses movie quotes in empty states, but some of them can read as alarming, or as if placing blame on the user (e.g. "Houston, we have a problem"). To keep Letterboxd's personality while improving usability, I introduced a standardized empty-state pattern.

Recommendation 03

A voice and tone optimized for localization

I developed foundational guidelines to help Letterboxd grow into a truly global platform. By standardizing the English UX copy first, Letterboxd can expand into additional languages with consistency and quality.

  • Clear, concise microcopy that avoids idioms and culturally specific humor
  • Labels, instructions, and errors that translate reliably
Learnings

Working on this project deepened my understanding of how intertwined UX writing and interaction design are. Something as small as one ambiguous label can disrupt the entire user journey. I also saw how personality-driven brands like Letterboxd must find a balance between voice and usability — too much personality can obscure meaning, but too little weakens the brand experience.

I gained a new appreciation for language as a design material. I had to consider not only what the copy conveyed, but how it would scale across contexts, screen sizes, and languages — which required thinking beyond English-speaking users, and imagining how these patterns might translate globally.
